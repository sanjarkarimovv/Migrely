Menstrual migraine: the day −2 to +3 window

Menstrual migraine means migraine attacks that happen from 2 days before a period to its 3rd day (days −2 to +3, where day 1 is the first day of bleeding and there is no day 0) in at least 2 of 3 cycles. If attacks happen only then, it is called pure menstrual migraine. If they also happen at other times, it is menstrually-related migraine, which is far more common. A doctor confirms it, usually from a diary kept over a few cycles.

What is menstrual migraine?

Menstrual migraine is migraine linked to periods: attacks from day −2 to day +3 in at least 2 of 3 cycles. In pure menstrual migraine, attacks happen only then. In menstrually-related migraine, they also happen at other times. The Migraine Trust says fewer than 1 in 100 women with migraine have the pure kind.

As many as two-thirds of women with migraine may have attacks linked to their period, the Migraine Trust says. The international classification, ICHD-3, sets out both kinds in its appendix of research criteria, because experts are not yet sure they are separate conditions. Most period attacks are without aura; forms with aura exist but are uncommon.

The difference matters for treatment: ICHD-3 notes that hormonal prevention is more likely to work for the pure kind. A doctor confirms which kind you have, based on your symptoms and your diary.

How do I count the days?

Day 1 is the first day of bleeding, and the day before it is day −1, so there is no day 0. The window is five days: days −2, −1, 1, 2 and 3. A withdrawal bleed in the break between combined-pill packs, or during cyclical hormone replacement therapy (HRT), counts as a period too.

If your bleeds come from the pill or HRT, say so in your diary: ICHD-3 notes that the cause of attacks may differ from a natural cycle, and so may the best way to manage them.

Why does migraine happen around a period?

A widely accepted explanation is the natural fall in oestrogen in the days before a period, which makes an attack more likely. Prostaglandins, hormone-like substances released around a period that also cause period pain, may add to the risk. The evidence is still limited, a 2023 review says, and other changes at this point in the cycle may matter too.

Oestrogen seems to act directly on pain pathways in the nervous system, the Migraine Trust says, though how it does so is not fully understood. The American Migraine Foundation says it is often the rapid change in hormone levels, rather than oestrogen itself, that matters. A 2021 review in The Lancet Neurology names two mechanisms, oestrogen withdrawal and prostaglandin release, and says treatments aimed at them might work, but the evidence is not robust.

Anything that changes oestrogen levels may affect migraine, the Migraine Trust says, including the contraceptive pill, HRT and the menopause, covered below.

Are period attacks worse than other migraine attacks?

Often, yes. The NHS says menstrual migraine attacks are often longer and more severe than attacks at other times. The Migraine Trust adds that they may come with more nausea or vomiting and sensitivity to light or sound, and may be harder to treat. They are mostly without aura.

A 2021 review in The Lancet Neurology says that, in women diagnosed with menstrual migraine, attacks around the period cause substantially more disability than their other attacks. The American Migraine Foundation adds that they can come back after first treatment. Note in your diary how your period attacks behave: it may help your doctor work out whether you have menstrual migraine and which treatment is best.

How long should I track it?

UK guidance (NICE) suggests a headache diary for at least 2 menstrual cycles, and the Migraine Trust at least 3 months. Mark your period days and every attack. NICE suggests suspecting migraine linked to periods when attacks happen mostly from day −2 to day +3 in at least 2 of 3 cycles in a row.

Write things down as they happen, not from memory: ICHD-3 notes that many women over-report a link between attacks and periods, and for research it asks for a diary kept over at least three cycles. The NHS suggests seeing a GP if you regularly get attacks around your period. The free printable headache diary has a column for period days.

What should I bring to my appointment?

Bring your diary, covering at least 2 menstrual cycles, with period days and every attack marked. Note how long and how bad each attack was, other symptoms, and every medicine you took, including over-the-counter ones. Also say whether you use hormonal contraception or HRT, and whether you are planning a pregnancy.

NICE suggests a diary also records possible triggers, and the American Migraine Foundation adds the first day of each period. Mention any aura, and say if it is new. Questions you might ask:

  • Is this menstrual migraine, and which kind?
  • Which options fit my periods and my contraception?
  • How can I keep my medicine days down? (Why medicine days matter)

How is it treated?

Standard migraine medicines can be used for menstrual migraine, the Migraine Trust says. For period-related migraine that is predictable and does not respond well to usual acute treatment, NICE describes options a doctor may consider on the days migraine is expected. Days you take medicine for it count toward medication-overuse headache. Talk to your doctor about what suits you.

Which options fit depends on you: the Migraine Trust says they depend on how regular your periods are, whether they are painful or heavy, and whether you also need contraception. It is also worth taking extra care with other triggers around your period, such as not drinking enough, disturbed sleep and irregular or missed meals.

Does contraception matter?

It can. NICE advises doctors not to routinely offer combined hormonal contraception (the combined pill, patch or ring) for contraception to people who have migraine with aura. If you have aura, or get it for the first time while using one of these methods, tell whoever prescribes your contraception soon. They may suggest a different method.

The reason is stroke risk. The Migraine Trust says studies suggest the oestrogen in these methods brings a small but significant rise in stroke risk with aura: still a very small risk, but an unnecessary one to take. With other stroke risk factors, such as smoking, obesity or high blood pressure, you may be advised not to use them either. It says progestogen-only methods (the mini-pill, implant, injection or hormonal coil) and non-hormonal methods can be used with aura. Whenever you see anyone about contraception, tell them you have migraine. If you stop a method, it no longer protects you from pregnancy.

Contraception can also change migraine itself, for better or worse, and the effect is hard to predict. The usual 7-day break in the combined pill brings a sharp fall in oestrogen that can trigger attacks in some people, and a doctor may suggest changes, such as fewer or shorter breaks.

Does menstrual migraine change in perimenopause and after menopause?

Often, yes. In perimenopause, the years before periods stop, hormone levels swing and attacks can become more frequent and severe, the Migraine Trust says. After menopause, migraine often gets better, the NHS says. The Migraine Trust adds that this can take a few years, and that it is more likely to continue or worsen when menopause follows surgery.

Period-linked attacks can become harder to predict as periods change, the Migraine Trust says. Hot flushes, night sweats and anxiety can disrupt sleep and add stress, which makes attacks more likely, and migraine can even start for the first time in perimenopause. The NHS lists headaches and migraines that are worse than usual among the symptoms of perimenopause and menopause. Keep the diary going, add your menopausal symptoms, and show it to your GP.

Can I take HRT if I have migraine?

The Migraine Trust says having migraine, including migraine with aura, does not mean you can't take HRT. HRT uses a different form of oestrogen, at a much lower dose than the combined pill, so the stroke risk is much lower. HRT helps migraine for some people and makes it worse for others.

Doctors usually recommend patches, gel or spray rather than tablets for people with migraine, the Migraine Trust says, because they give steadier hormone levels, at the lowest dose that controls symptoms. If HRT seems to make migraine worse, tell your doctor: the type or dose can be reviewed. Your doctor will go through the benefits and risks before prescribing.

Is my period data private in Migrely?

Migrely has no internet access and no account, so it cannot send your period days or migraines anywhere. They are kept on your phone, and can leave it only in two ways: in Android's own backup to your Google account, if backup is on (on Android 9 and later only end-to-end encrypted, which needs a screen lock), and in any file you export and share. Tracking periods is optional: turning it off hides the marks, and any you saved stay until you delete them. You can check the privacy yourself.

Marking period days on the calendar is free. Once the months you look at include at least 2 period starts and 5 migraines, Insights, part of Premium, counts how many of your migraines started in these five days and shows how much of the time those days make up. It doesn't draw a conclusion: that is for you and your doctor.

When should I get help straight away?

Call your local emergency number if a headache comes with any of these:

  • Extremely bad pain that came on suddenly
  • Weakness or loss of movement on one side of your body or face
  • Problems speaking or remembering things
  • Losing your vision, or blurred or double vision
  • Feeling drowsy or confused
  • A seizure or fit
  • A very high temperature and signs of meningitis, such as a stiff neck
  • A rash that doesn't fade when you press a glass against it (this may be harder to see on brown or black skin)
  • A head injury in the last 3 months

Don't drive yourself to hospital. Call your local emergency number.

Get urgent medical advice if you have a migraine attack and:

  • It has lasted longer than 72 hours
  • Aura symptoms last longer than 1 hour at a time
  • You're pregnant or have just had a baby

If you have any other symptoms you're worried about, get urgent medical advice.

These lists follow the NHS migraine, headaches and meningitis pages. In the UK, urgent medical advice means an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111.
