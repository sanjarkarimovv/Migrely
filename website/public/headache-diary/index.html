Headache diary: what to write down, with a free printable template

A headache diary turns hazy weeks into facts your doctor can use. Here is what UK guidance suggests recording, for how long, and a free 8-week template to print.

Why keep a headache diary?

NICE, which writes guidance for doctors in England, suggests considering a headache diary to help diagnose headaches. A diary also shows whether a treatment is working, and gives you and your doctor something concrete to talk about. The Migraine Trust adds that it can show patterns in your attacks and help you recognise triggers and warning signs.

What should I write down?

For each headache, write the date, when it started and stopped, how bad it was and any other symptoms, such as nausea, or light or sound bothering you. Add every medicine you took for it, prescribed or bought, and whether it helped. NICE also suggests noting possible triggers and any link to your period.

NICE's list in full:

  • how often headaches come, how long they last and how bad they get;
  • other symptoms, such as nausea, or light or sound bothering you;
  • every medicine you take for them, prescribed and over the counter;
  • possible triggers;
  • any link to your period, if you have periods.

Rate how bad each headache was as mild, moderate or severe, or on a scale up to 10, where 10 is the worst pain you can imagine.

Which possible triggers should I note?

Note changes from your usual routine: sleep, meals, caffeine and alcohol, stress, the weather, your period, and bright light or noise. But a trigger seen alongside an attack is not proof that it caused it. Some “triggers” are early symptoms of the attack itself, and several factors often act together.

  • Early symptoms can look like triggers. Hours or up to 2 days before the pain, some people crave certain foods or become more sensitive to light. If you eat chocolate because of a craving, chocolate can look like a trigger when it isn't.
  • Factors add up. One thing on its own may be less likely to cause an attack, but two or three together may. A real trigger may not cause an attack every time.
  • Beliefs don't always hold up. In a 2013 lab study, 27 people with migraine with aura who said bright or flickering light or hard exercise set off their attacks were exposed to them. 6 of the 27 had a migraine attack afterwards.

So don't cut things out on a hunch: the Migraine Trust warns that focusing on triggers too much can do more harm than good. A diary may also show that something you blamed matters less than you thought.

How long should I keep it?

At least 8 weeks, NICE suggests. If headaches seem linked to your period, keep it for at least 2 menstrual cycles; the Migraine Trust suggests at least 3 months (more on menstrual migraine). For frequent headaches, the international classification (ICHD-3) says a day-by-day diary for at least a month is generally needed.

How do I keep a headache diary going?

Keep it simple, and write in it every day rather than from memory later. In a 2020 study, people remembered fewer headache days at the end of each month than their daily diaries showed. Paper, a phone app or a spreadsheet all work: the best one is the one you will keep using.

  • Keep it short. The Migraine Trust says basic information is often best. If the diary starts to feel overwhelming, the American Migraine Foundation suggests asking your doctor how to simplify it.
  • Write it on the day. That 2020 study used a US trial of 182 people with migraine. A monthly questionnaire found fewer headache days than a daily diary in every 4-week period: 11.1 against 13.7 on average.
  • Daily is the default. ICHD-3 says frequent headaches generally need a day-by-day record. Your doctor may prefer something simpler, such as entries only on days with symptoms, the American Migraine Foundation says.

What counts as a headache day?

Any day with a headache counts as a headache day, whether the headache lasted all day or only for part of it. That is the definition in the international classification (ICHD-3), and doctors usually count your headache days over a month.

Fill in a line on pain-free days too, so a blank line never leaves you guessing whether it was a good day or a forgotten one.

What if I have more than one kind of headache?

You can have more than one kind, such as migraine and tension-type headache, and each is diagnosed separately. The international classification (ICHD-3) highly recommends a diary that records the main features of every headache. It helps a doctor tell the kinds apart and count each one, and helps you learn the difference yourself.

For each headache, note:

  • where the pain is;
  • what it feels like, for example throbbing or pressing;
  • how bad it is;
  • whether routine activity makes it worse;
  • other symptoms;
  • how long it lasts.

These are the features NICE uses to tell the main kinds apart, and your doctor makes the diagnosis. A 2007 Danish study comparing diaries with clinical interviews found the diary valuable for telling migraine from tension-type headache. Keep a row for every headache day, whatever the kind: for chronic migraine, migraine-like and tension-type-like days are counted together.

Why count medicine days?

Taking medicine for attacks on many days a month can itself make headaches more frequent. This is called medication-overuse headache. The international classification (ICHD-3) counts the days a month you take each kind of medicine, not the number of doses, so a day with two doses counts once.

ICHD-3 describes it in people who have headache on 15 or more days a month after more than 3 months of taking:

  • triptans, ergots, opioids or combination painkillers (such as those with caffeine or codeine) on 10 or more days a month;
  • a mix of these kinds, or of these and simple painkillers, on 10 or more days a month in total;
  • simple painkillers alone, such as paracetamol, aspirin or ibuprofen, one or several, on 15 or more days a month.

These counts are for diagnosis; the everyday advice is lower: the NHS suggests not taking painkillers on more than 2 days a week. Don't stop or change medicines without talking to your doctor. More on medication-overuse headache.

What does a doctor look for in a headache diary?

A doctor can use your diary to see what kind of headache you have, how many headache days you have a month, how often you take medicine for them, and whether treatment is helping. The counts matter because diagnoses such as chronic migraine and medication-overuse headache are defined by days a month.

  • How often. Migraine on fewer than 15 days a month is called episodic. Chronic migraine means headache on 15 or more days a month for more than 3 months, with migraine features on at least 8 days a month (NICE, ICHD-3).
  • Medicine days. ICHD-3 notes that medication overuse is the most common cause of symptoms that look like chronic migraine, so the two counts are read together. A day with two kinds of medicine counts under each kind, and once in the total of days with any medicine.
  • Whether treatment works. NICE suggests reviewing preventive treatment 3 to 6 months after it starts, and for some preventive treatments it links continuing them to how far migraines or headache days fall.

Before an appointment, add up each month's headache days and medicine days by kind, and list your questions. Page 6 of the template has boxes for these counts.

When should I get help straight away?

Call your local emergency number if a headache comes with any of these:

  • Extremely bad pain that came on suddenly
  • Weakness or loss of movement on one side of your body or face
  • Problems speaking or remembering things
  • Losing your vision, or blurred or double vision
  • Feeling drowsy or confused
  • A seizure or fit
  • A very high temperature and signs of meningitis, such as a stiff neck
  • A rash that doesn't fade when you press a glass against it
  • A head injury in the last 3 months

Don't drive yourself to hospital. Call your local emergency number.

Get urgent medical advice if you have a migraine attack and:

  • It has lasted longer than 72 hours
  • Aura symptoms last longer than 1 hour at a time
  • You're pregnant or have just had a baby

If you have any other symptoms you're worried about, get urgent medical advice.

These lists follow the NHS migraine and headaches pages. In the UK, urgent medical advice means an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111.
