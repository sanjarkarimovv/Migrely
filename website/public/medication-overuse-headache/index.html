Medication-overuse headache: the 10- and 15-day rules

Medication-overuse headache is headache on 15 or more days a month that comes from taking medicine for headache too often for more than 3 months: on 10 or more days a month for triptans, ergots, opioids, combination painkillers or a mix of kinds, or on 15 or more days a month for simple painkillers alone, one or several.

What is medication-overuse headache?

Medication-overuse headache is headache on 15 or more days a month that comes from taking medicine for headache too often, in someone who already has a headache disorder such as migraine. It used to be called rebound headache. Doctors class it as a secondary headache, meaning one with another cause, and it can be diagnosed alongside chronic migraine.

Medicine that eases an attack, taken on many days, can make headaches more frequent. The American Migraine Foundation describes the cycle: more pain leads to more medicine, and the medicine itself can then worsen the headache and how often it comes.

How common is it, and who gets it?

About 1 to 2 in every 100 people have medication-overuse headache in a given year, a 2019 review in The Lancet Neurology says. It is more common in women and in people with depression, anxiety or other long-term pain. Most people who get it already have migraine or tension-type headache, ICHD-3 says.

It is very common among people with frequent headache: ICHD-3 says studies from many countries show that more than half of people with headache on 15 or more days a month have it. A 2017 review puts it at 3 to 4 times as common in women as in men.

What are the signs?

The main sign is headache on most days while you are taking medicine for headache often. The Migraine Trust describes a dull headache on most days, with migraine attacks on top, and pain that comes back as each dose wears off. Only a doctor can diagnose it, because its headaches have no unique features.

UK guidance (NICE) asks doctors to think of it when headache started or got worse while someone took medicine for headache on 10 or 15 days a month, depending on the kind, for 3 months or more. Other sources mention:

  • preventive treatment that works less well than expected (the Migraine Trust);
  • taking medicine in case a headache comes, rather than for one you already have (a 2017 review);
  • needing medicine on more than 2 days a week as headaches become more frequent, which the American Migraine Foundation calls a reason to see your doctor.

If this sounds familiar, see a GP. Take a record of your headache days and of every medicine you take for them, prescribed or bought, plus any you take for other pain. The American Migraine Foundation stresses that it is not your fault: overuse can be a sign that migraine is not fully managed.

How many days count as overuse?

ICHD-3, the international classification, counts days a month, kept up for more than 3 months. Triptans, ergots, opioids and combination painkillers count from 10 days a month, and so does a mix of kinds in total. Simple painkillers alone, such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, count from 15 days a month.

Simple painkillers are one kind, so paracetamol on some days and ibuprofen on others still count toward 15, not 10.

Does it count doses or days?

Days, not doses. ICHD-3 counts the days each month you take a medicine. A day with two doses counts once. A day with a triptan and paracetamol counts once for each kind, and once in the total. Days you take these medicines for other pain, such as period or back pain, count too.

How do these counts compare with everyday advice?

The everyday advice is lower. These are the counts doctors use to diagnose it, not a safe amount. The NHS suggests not taking painkillers on more than 2 days a week, and NICE asks doctors to explain the risk to anyone using medicine for headache attacks.

ICHD-3 itself says its day counts come from expert opinion rather than formal evidence.

How can I lower my risk?

The NHS suggests trying not to take painkillers on more than 2 days a week, because more can cause more headaches and make migraine harder to treat. Count the days you take medicine for any pain, not just headache. If attacks are frequent, ask a doctor about preventive treatment, so fewer attacks need treating.

For very frequent attacks, the Migraine Trust says a preventive treatment is usually recommended, because fewer attacks mean fewer painkillers. NICE asks doctors to talk through the benefits and risks of preventive treatment for migraine, including how much headaches affect your life. The American Migraine Foundation notes that preventive medicines have not been linked to medication-overuse headache. The NHS suggests seeing a GP if you have migraine attacks more than once a week.

How is it treated?

UK guidance (NICE) says treatment means stopping the overused medicine for at least a month; the Migraine Trust says to agree a plan with your doctor first. Headaches are likely to get worse before they improve, and there may be withdrawal symptoms, so doctors offer close follow-up and support, and a review 4 to 8 weeks after stopping.

Doctors may also offer preventive treatment for the underlying headache. NICE says a hospital stay is not routinely needed. People who take strong opioids, have other relevant health conditions, or have tried to stop several times without success may need a specialist, and sometimes a hospital stay. The best way to stop depends on the medicines and on your health, the Migraine Trust says, and the American Migraine Foundation warns that stopping some medicines suddenly can be dangerous.

Plan this with your doctor, especially if you take opioids, and don't stop or change medicines on your own.

How long do headaches get worse after stopping?

Often for 1 to 2 weeks, though it varies: a 2017 review found many people have withdrawal symptoms for that long, most often a worse headache at first. The American Migraine Foundation says the first few weeks tend to be the worst, and fully breaking the cycle may take 2 to 6 months.

Other withdrawal symptoms can include nausea, poor sleep, restlessness, and an upset stomach or diarrhoea. The Migraine Trust says they can be hardest after ergotamine, triptans and codeine- or morphine-based medicines, and milder after paracetamol or anti-inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen. It lists things that can help: regular rest and sleep, drinking enough fluids, avoiding caffeine, doing less where you can, and hot or cold packs. Ask your doctor what support suits you.

Will it go away, and can it come back?

It usually gets better once the overuse stops, though not always, ICHD-3 says, and preventive treatment tends to work better afterwards. It can come back: a 2017 review says most studies found 25 to 35% of people relapsed within the first year after stopping, and few relapsed after that.

ICHD-3 notes that around half of people who seem to have chronic migraine while overusing medicine go back to episodic migraine after stopping it. The same 2017 review says around 50 to 70% of people seem to respond well to stopping, that relapse figures vary widely, from 20 to 60%, and that most studies were done in specialist headache centres, so they may not reflect everyone.

When should I get help straight away?

Call your local emergency number if a headache comes with any of these:

  • Extremely bad pain that came on suddenly
  • Weakness or loss of movement on one side of your body or face
  • Problems speaking or remembering things
  • Losing your vision, or blurred or double vision
  • Feeling drowsy or confused
  • A seizure or fit
  • A very high temperature and signs of meningitis, such as a stiff neck
  • A rash that doesn't fade when you press a glass against it (this may be harder to see on brown or black skin)
  • A head injury in the last 3 months

Don't drive yourself to hospital. Call your local emergency number.

Get urgent medical advice if you have a migraine attack and:

  • It has lasted longer than 72 hours
  • Aura symptoms last longer than 1 hour at a time
  • You're pregnant or have just had a baby

If you have any other symptoms you're worried about, get urgent medical advice.

These lists follow the NHS migraine and headaches pages. In the UK, urgent medical advice means an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111.
